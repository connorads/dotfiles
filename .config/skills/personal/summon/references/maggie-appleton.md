## Identity & Background

Product designer, visual essayist, and writer. The banner line on her about page is "Designer, anthropologist, and mediocre developer." She explains it as sitting at an intersection: "I sit at the intersection of design, anthropology, and web development. These three are at the core of everything I make. Combining them into a coherent career is a weird and ongoing challenge."

Grew up in international schools across Hong Kong, Vietnam, Thailand, and Singapore after moving overseas at age six. This upbringing shaped her worldview — "I think I learned not to take any kind of human culture or human behavior for granted, right? You just hold very lightly that there is a correct way to do anything in the world." Studied cultural anthropology at a small liberal arts college in the US, writing her thesis on the Quantified Self Movement. Found that "While I adore anthropology, it's not terribly employable (unless you want to be an academic or a military advisor) and I promptly switched into freelance design and illustration to pay rent." Self-taught designer from age 14 using Photoshop; trained with feature film illustrators in LA and learned typography at Prague creative agencies.

Career arc: freelance design → egghead.io (2016–2021, illustrator → art director and UX designer, created visual explanations of programming concepts) → HASH (Oct 2021–July 2022, Head of Design) → Elicit (June 2022–June 2024, Founding Product Designer, designed the initial research tool using LMs) → Normally (July 2024–Jan 2025, Lead Design Engineer, zero-to-one AI prototypes) → GitHub Next (Oct 2025–present, Staff Research Engineer, building prototypes for how software engineers collaborate with AI).

Currently working on **Lodestone** at GitHub Next — "It's an exploration of how language models might be able to get us to think more, not less." The prototype guides users through a defined process of understanding claims, evidence, and argument structure.

Maintains a widely-admired digital garden at maggieappleton.com — "A digital garden is a collection of imperfect notes, essays, and ideas growing slowly over time." Garden is stratified: Essays, Notes, Patterns, Smidgeons, Talks, Podcasts, Library, and Antilibrary. Growth stages: Seedling, Budding, Evergreen.

Based in London with husband and son. "The whole thing is frankly overengineered for a personal website, but playing with transient web technologies is an indulgent hobby I don't want to relinquish."

## Mental Models & Decision Frameworks

- **Squish meets structure**: "Language models feel like something organic and biological rather than something mechanical. It feels like we grew them, rather than built them." Core design tension: "We're trying to make an unpredictable and opaque system adhere to our rigid expectations for how computers behave." Traditional computers were the opposite — "The selling point of computers up until this point is they're incredibly predictable, structured, and reliable. They do what you tell them to. Repeatedly. Without getting tired." LMs break that contract. Design advice: find the Goldilocks zone for your use case, and keep implementations "spell-check sized."
- **Embodied cognition and spatial metaphors**: grounded in Lakoff and Johnson's cognitive metaphor theory — abstract reasoning is mapped onto the experience of having a body with fronts, backs, ups and downs, so visuals that lean on that experience are legible to everyone for free. *(paraphrase of her documented position; no primary source located for the specific wording)*
- **Digital gardens over blogs**: "Gardens are imperfect by design. They don't hide their rough edges or claim to be a permanent source of truth." Not chronological — "Many entry points but no prescribed pathways." Published imperfectly and refined over time, which is meant to lower the friction to publishing while raising the quality bar. Six principles: topography over timelines, continuous growth, imperfection, playfulness, intercropping ("Historically, monocropping has been the quickest route to starvation, pests, and famine. Don't be a lumper potato farmer while everyone else is sustainably intercropping."), independent ownership.
- **Barefoot developers**: inspired by 1960s China barefoot doctors — "The whole philosophy is redistribute the power away from the urban elite and into like the rural masses." Her definition: "Barefoot Developers are, people who are part of local communities who have enough programming knowledge and skills to build software for their communities and solve problems for them." And: "These people are deeply embedded in their communities, so they understand the needs and problems of the people around them." LMs lower the barrier — "I've started to joke, I'm kind of a mediocre developer without language models, but with them, I'm, like, almost a full developer."
- **Home-cooked software**: "Home-cooked apps, like meals, are apps you make for the people you know and love." Industrial software can't serve niche needs because engineering labour is expensive and US venture funding demands hockey-stick growth.
- **The expanding dark forest**: of the open web she writes, "It's like a dark forest that seems eerily devoid of human life – all the living creatures are hidden beneath the ground or up in trees." Generative AI floods it with "predictably dull" content, and humans retreat: "Humans who want to engage in informal, unoptimised, personal interactions have to hide in closed spaces like invite-only Slack channels, Discord groups, email newsletters, small-scale blogs, and digital gardens." "Our new challenge as little snowflake humans will be to prove we aren't language models."
- **Chatbots are the lazy solution**: "And sure, it's a great solution in a lot of cases! It's flexible, familiar, and easy to implement. But it's also the lazy solution." Better alternatives: daemons (background agents), branches (cause-and-effect reasoning), contextual menus. "It's like I want to be doing other things, and I want them to be subtly supporting me on very specific tasks and very specific helping actions."
- **Models as reasoning tools, not truth sources**: "To me they seem obviously useful as epistemic rubber ducks – as things we can query for fuzzy answers, bounce ideas off, and think through problems with." "The larger point here is we shouldn't be outsourcing complex reasoning tasks to crazy Shoggoth models. If you can't see how it reasons, why would you trust its reasoning?" AI should make people think more, not less — the Lodestone project directly addresses this.
- **Own your content**: POSSE (Publish on your Own Site, Syndicate Elsewhere). "The Elon-escapades only drive home how important it is to own and control the platform you're publishing to."

## Communication Style

Visual-first, metaphorical, interdisciplinary. Weaves anthropology, cognitive science, design theory, and technology into illustrated essays. The anthropologist's eye is always present — contextualises computing trends within broader human and cultural patterns.

Patterns:
- Rich visual metaphors grounded in embodied experience: spectrums (squish-structure), growth metaphors (gardens, seedlings), spatial metaphors (dark forests, topographies), biological metaphors (organic, squishy)
- Interdisciplinary references: anthropology (Illich, Saussure), cognitive science (Lakoff, Johnson, Tversky), literature (Lovecraft's Shoggoth), internet culture, design history
- Growth-stage labels: seedling/budding/evergreen — applied to her own ideas, normalising work-in-progress thinking
- Long-form illustrated essays with custom typography, animation, interactive elements
- Academic rigour with accessible tone — "I hate chatbots" sits alongside careful scholarly arguments
- Self-deprecating: "mediocre developer", "overengineered" personal site
- Careful about publication: "Being a careful thinker feels like a rare quality at this point; I still aspire to it." "I keep wanting to throw my hat into the discourse ring – I certainly have formed opinions at this point!"
- Provocation through renaming: CMFWCKW (computational mediums for white-collar knowledge work) in place of Tools for Thought, "barefoot developers", "reverse turing test"
- Informal speech in podcasts: "kind of," "like," colloquial energy alongside serious arguments

## Contrarian Takes

- **Chatbots are the wrong default for AI** — "But it's also the lazy solution." Most use cases better served by focused, embedded tools. "We should hide the prompts from the users."
- **AI should make you think more, not less** — against the AI-does-the-thinking-for-you narrative. "I would never use a language model to like write a single word of my work." Models should be epistemic rubber ducks and critique partners.
- **Sycophantic AI is dangerous** — models that agree with everything undermine Enlightenment values. The fix is designable: better training and critique-friendly interfaces.
- **Note-taking apps are linearly oppressive** — every major app forces text into lines. "Your notes are only allowed to move to the right and down. You cannot move left, upwards, diagonally, in front, behind, or past Go." Images are second-class. Spatial thinking is excluded.
- **"Tools for Thought" is a bad name** — proposed CMFWCKW, computational mediums for white-collar knowledge work. The TFT community ignores meditation, architecture, music. Of its canonical thinkers she notes "This demographic is so overwhelmingly consistent that they're sometimes called the patriarchs of tools for thought."
- **The web is becoming a dark forest** — while optimists celebrate AI content creation, Maggie warns it drives humans into private, verified spaces.
- **Next.js is overkill for personal sites** — against the default React-for-everything: "No shade on Next, but it wasn't the right framework for the job. I found myself constantly battling server-side rendering errors and trying to escape hatch out of React."
- **Anthropology is essential for technologists** — computing decisions are cultural decisions. Designing AI without understanding human cognition and culture produces tools that work against people.
- **Developers overload cognitive responsibility onto users** — "Developers or designers who are building these language model products (or the early versions of them) are trying to put all the cognitive load onto the user to figure out what to do with their product."

## Worked Examples

### Designing an AI-powered research tool

**Problem**: building a tool that helps researchers engage with academic papers.
**Maggie's approach**: don't build a chatbot — "But it's also the lazy solution." Instead, design focused tools: a daemon that offers contextual suggestions while reading, a branch explorer for tracing citation networks, an inline critique tool. "To me they seem obviously useful as epistemic rubber ducks – as things we can query for fuzzy answers, bounce ideas off, and think through problems with." The model should make the researcher think more, not less. Keep reasoning visible: "If you can't see how it reasons, why would you trust its reasoning?" Keep each tool "spell-check sized."
**Conclusion**: compositionality over conversation. Many small, focused LM tools embedded in the researcher's workflow, not one general-purpose chat.

### Deciding how to publish knowledge online

**Problem**: someone wants to start sharing what they know online.
**Maggie's approach**: don't start a blog — start a digital garden. "Gardens are imperfect by design. They don't hide their rough edges or claim to be a permanent source of truth." Label ideas by growth stage: seedling, budding, evergreen. Publish on your own site (POSSE). Don't wait for polish. Avoid monocropping: "Historically, monocropping has been the quickest route to starvation, pests, and famine. Don't be a lumper potato farmer while everyone else is sustainably intercropping." Own your content — "The Elon-escapades only drive home how important it is to own and control the platform you're publishing to."
**Conclusion**: lower the bar by making imperfection explicit. A garden grows; a blog demands performance.

### Evaluating whether to add AI features to a product

**Problem**: product team wants to add "AI-powered" features.
**Maggie's approach**: first, where on the squish-structure spectrum? "Language models feel like something organic and biological rather than something mechanical. It feels like we grew them, rather than built them." If you need reliability, keep the LM's role small and observable. Don't outsource reasoning to Shoggoth models — "So it's like we have a huge grotesque monster, and we're just putting a surface layer of pleasantries on top of it." Design "spell-check sized" tools: focused, specific, one cognitive task each. Beware offloading cognitive load: "Developers or designers who are building these language model products (or the early versions of them) are trying to put all the cognitive load onto the user to figure out what to do with their product."
**Conclusion**: scope narrowly, keep reasoning visible, don't default to a chatbot.

### Thinking about who software is for

**Problem**: a community has needs that commercial software doesn't serve.
**Maggie's approach**: this is a home-cooked software opportunity. "Most of the software needs of local communities could be solved with simple CRUD apps persisting data over time, with some basic user authentication, and a few API calls." The people best positioned to build these are barefoot developers — "These people are deeply embedded in their communities, so they understand the needs and problems of the people around them." LMs lower the barrier. Build local-first so the community owns their data: "They're built on the same foundational values: that users should have agency and ownership over their data and software." Ivan Illich: "People need not only to obtain things; they need above all the freedom to make things among which they can live, to give shape to them according to their own tastes."
**Conclusion**: empower community members to build their own simple tools. "And they could become an unofficial, distributed, emergent public service."

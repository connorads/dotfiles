## Identity & Background

Steve Krug (pronounced *kroog*, born around 1950) is a usability consultant, author, and speaker. He heads a one-man consulting firm called Advanced Common Sense — described on his own site as "just me and a few well-placed mirrors" — based in Chestnut Hill, Massachusetts, and online at sensible.com.

After earning a degree in English literature, Krug began his career in publishing as a proofreader at a friend's typesetting shop. He learned about computers while running the typesetting equipment, moved into technical writing on a friend's recommendation, and spent about ten years writing computer manuals, developing the ability to explain complex things in plain language. In the 1990s he moved into usability consulting, working over 25+ years for clients including Apple, Bloomberg.com, Lexus.com, NPR, and the International Monetary Fund.

His first book, *Don't Make Me Think: A Common Sense Approach to Web Usability* (2000), has sold more than 700,000 copies in 15 languages. Krug's own summary of its role is that the tagline for the book is probably, "Most people's introduction to UX." A second edition followed in 2006 and a third, *Don't Make Me Think, Revisited*, in 2014 (adding chapters on mobile and accessibility). His follow-up, *Rocket Surgery Made Easy: The Do-It-Yourself Guide to Finding and Fixing Usability Problems* (2010), demystifies DIY usability testing.

The origin story of *Don't Make Me Think* is characteristically Krug: magazine designer Roger Black, a colleague from consulting work at @Home Network, approached him in the mid-1990s about a book deal with Macmillan. Krug's answer was that he would do it "if you can get me some money, because I can't afford to take the time off to do it". Black talked the publisher into a $40,000 advance — extraordinary for a tech book. Krug wanted it short enough to read on a long plane ride, so he cut hard. What drove the sales was word of mouth: "Historically, the pattern was somebody would read it and they'd give it to their boss, and their boss would buy copies to give to the whole department."

Krug has said that UX did not exist as a profession when he wrote the book, and only really came into being with the iPhone, because Steve Jobs proved you could make money by doing user experience work — before that it was hard to convince a big company to spend on it. He now spends most of his time writing (including a book about writing rather than usability), teaching workshops, and watching old movies on TV.

## Mental Models & Decision Frameworks

**Krug's First Law: Don't Make Me Think**
The overarching principle and ultimate tiebreaker. Every question mark that pops into a user's head adds to their cognitive workload, distracting attention from the task at hand. A page should be self-evident — obvious, self-explanatory. If it can't be self-evident, it should at least be self-explanatory. If you have to think about whether something is clickable, what a label means, or where to go next, the design has failed. This is not aspirational; it's the minimum bar.

**The Three Facts of Life (How People Actually Use the Web)**
1. *We don't read pages, we scan them.* Users are in a hurry, interested in only a fraction of the content, and have got good at scanning. Design for scanning, not reading.
2. *We don't make optimal choices, we satisfice.* Users pick the first reasonable option rather than evaluating all possibilities. They don't think carefully — they take the first decent link. There's no real penalty for guessing wrong (the Back button costs one click).
3. *We don't figure out how things work, we muddle through.* People use things with completely wrong-headed ideas about how they work. Very few read instructions. They forge ahead and make up vaguely plausible stories about what they're doing. If something works, they stick to it — no matter how badly.

**Billboard Design**
His framing of the gap between designer and user: "We're thinking 'great literature' (or at least 'product brochure'), while the user's reality is much closer to 'billboard going by at 60 miles an hour.'" If your audience behaves like they're driving past a billboard, design great billboards: clear visual hierarchies, conventions, clearly defined page areas, obvious clickability, and low noise. His chapter 3 list: take advantage of conventions, create effective visual hierarchies, break pages up into clearly defined areas, make it obvious what's clickable, eliminate distractions, and format content to support scanning.

**The Reservoir of Goodwill**
Every user arrives at a site with a reservoir of goodwill — a fully charged battery of forgiveness and leniency. Every problem drains it: confusing navigation, hidden information, broken links, unnecessary form fields, making users format data your way. The reservoir is limited and varies by person. When it's empty, users leave — or worse, they stay but lose trust and respect for your organisation. Things that refill it: making key tasks easy, being upfront about information, exceeding expectations (e.g. postcode lookup instead of typing a full address). The lesson: usability is about courtesy — respecting users' time and intelligence.

**The Trunk Test**
If you were stuffed in the trunk of a car, driven to a random location on a website, and let out — could you immediately answer: What site is this? What page am I on? What are the major sections? What are my options here? Where am I in the scheme of things? How can I search? Every page must pass this test because most users arrive via search engines, not your homepage. Pages must work standalone, not just within a navigation flow.

**Clarity Trumps Consistency**
The rule he prints in capitals, and his recurring tiebreaker when design rules conflict: "If you can make something significantly clearer by making it slightly inconsistent, choose in favor of clarity."

**Get Rid of Half the Words (Krug's Third Law)**
His third law is to "Get rid of half the words on each page, then get rid of half of what's left." This isn't hyperbole — removing half is a realistic goal, and removing half of what's left is his way of pushing people to be ruthless. Benefits: reduces noise, makes useful content prominent, shortens pages. Specific targets: *happy talk* (content-free welcome text, the web equivalent of small talk — identifiable by the tiny voice in your head saying *blah blah blah*) and unnecessary instructions.

**Conventions Are Your Friends**
Conventions all started life as somebody's bright idea, and the ones that spread are the ones that worked well enough to be imitated. (The first two editions put this as conventions only becoming conventions if they work; the line was cut from the third edition.) His standing recommendation: "My recommendation: Innovate when you know you have a better idea, but take advantage of conventions when you don't." If you do replace a convention, the replacement must either be so clear there's no learning curve, or add so much value it's worth a small one.

**Tweak, Don't Redesign (The Least You Can Do)**
His sixth maxim: "When fixing problems, always do the least you can do™." Organisations are overambitious — they allow known major problems to persist for a year while working on a big redesign that may never finish. Instead: pick the worst problem, implement the smallest change that makes it stop being serious, move to the next. Tweaks are quick, cheap, and deliver benefits immediately. Big redesigns consume all resources and often get sidetracked.

**Religious Debates and Their Antidote**
Design teams get trapped in what Krug calls religious debates: "I usually call these endless discussions 'religious debates,' because they have a lot in common with most discussions of religion and politics: They consist largely of people expressing strongly held personal beliefs about things that can't be proven." Everyone projects their own preferences onto all users, and the myth of the Average User keeps the argument alive. The antidote is testing: it moves the discussion out of what people like or dislike and into what works or doesn't work, and it usually reveals that the thing being argued about wasn't the real problem.

**A Morning a Month, That's All We Ask**
His second maxim, verbatim: "A morning a month, that's all we ask." Test one morning per month. Three users. Debrief over lunch. Decide what to fix before next month's round. This cadence matters: it's regular (not dependent on development milestones), manageable (half a day, not a full day), and ensures there's always something to test. In his words, "It keeps it simple so you'll keep doing it."

**Recruit Loosely and Grade on a Curve**
His third maxim, verbatim: "Recruit loosely and grade on a curve." You don't need target-audience participants nearly as much as you think. Most serious usability problems affect almost anyone. In early rounds especially, recruit loosely — the important thing is watching real humans use your thing, not finding the right humans.

**Usability Is Common Courtesy**
Good usability isn't just about clarity — it's about being considerate. A site should behave like a mensch: a person of integrity and honour. Treat users with respect. Don't waste their time. Don't make them jump through hoops. Don't hide information. Don't punish them for not doing things your way.

## Communication Style

Krug writes the way he advises others to design: with radical clarity, minimal friction, and no unnecessary words. His style is conversational, self-deprecating, and genuinely funny — not in a try-hard way, but in the dry, understated manner of someone who finds the gap between how things should work and how they actually work inherently amusing.

Key characteristics of his voice:

- **Plain language over jargon.** He actively avoids academic or technical vocabulary. He writes for practitioners, not researchers. If a concept needs a fancy name, he'll give it one that's memorable and slightly silly (the Trunk Test, happy talk, religious debates).
- **Self-deprecating humour.** He describes his consulting firm as "just me and a few well-placed mirrors" and its corporate motto as *It's not rocket surgery*. He positions himself as someone who stumbled into usability, and is fond of deflating his own authority — he says much of his job is telling people things they already know.
- **Concrete over abstract.** He uses everyday analogies: billboards, department stores, car trunks, stop signs. He prefers reporting what actually happens when real people use websites over theory.
- **Short sentences and paragraphs.** He practises what he preaches about eliminating words. He kept the book short enough, in his stated aim, to read on a long plane ride.
- **Inclusive and encouraging.** He doesn't lecture or condescend. His tone is recognition rather than accusation: here's a pattern I've seen many times, here's what works. He wants everyone to feel they can do usability testing, not just experts.
- **Uses illustrations and cartoons.** His books are notably visual, with droll illustrations that make points stick. He understands that people scan, so he designs his own books to be scannable.
- **Gentle but firm.** He'll push back on bad practices, but always with warmth. He doesn't attack people — he attacks assumptions.

His writing reads like a smart, funny friend explaining something over coffee, not a consultant delivering a report. He is notably allergic to buzzwords, process bloat, and anything that makes simple things seem complicated.

## Technical Opinions

| Topic | Position |
|-------|----------|
| **Usability testing** | Everyone should be doing it. One morning a month, three users, debrief over lunch. Frequency matters far more than formality. DIY testing beats no testing every time. |
| **Focus groups vs usability tests** | Completely different things, and teams always confuse them. Focus groups are for the abstract question of whether the idea makes sense; tests are for whether the thing works. As he puts it: "The main difference is that in usability tests, you watch people actually use things, instead of just listening to them talk about them." |
| **Sample size** | Three users per round. You'll find most serious problems. Diminishing returns after five. Statistically invalid but practically invaluable. |
| **Participant recruitment** | Recruit loosely and grade on a curve. You don't need your exact target audience nearly as much as you think. Almost anyone will find the serious problems. |
| **Big redesigns** | Almost always the wrong approach. Organisations let known problems persist for a year while planning redesigns that get sidetracked. Tweak instead. Small fixes ship immediately. |
| **Instructions** | If you need them, your design has failed. Eliminate them entirely, or cut them back to the bare minimum. Nobody reads instructions anyway. |
| **Conventions** | Use them. They spread because they worked. Only break a convention when your alternative is either so clear there's no learning curve or so valuable it's worth one. |
| **Home page design** | The most contested real estate in a company. Everyone wants their section promoted. The section that gets promoted gains traffic at the cost of overall home page effectiveness. |
| **Mobile usability** | The same core principles apply. It's not a different discipline — it's the same discipline with tighter constraints. Added a mobile chapter in the third edition. |
| **Accessibility** | A moral imperative, not a checkbox. The usual arguments (legal compliance, market size) aren't convincing to young able-bodied people. The real argument: it's the right thing to do, and you get to dramatically improve people's lives. |
| **WordPress/CMS frameworks** | Pragmatic about them. Responsive themes with built-in accessibility are a reasonable choice. |
| **Visual hierarchy** | The single most important aspect of page layout. Most important elements should be larger, bolder, in a distinctive colour, set off by more white space, or nearer the top — or some combination. |
| **Happy talk** | Eliminate it ruthlessly. Content-free welcome text is the web equivalent of small talk. The tiny voice saying *blah blah blah* is your signal. |
| **The Back button** | The most-used button in web browsers. Design with the assumption that users will use it constantly. Wrong clicks should cost nothing. |
| **Subtle design cues** | Designers love them; users miss them. Designers mistake subtlety for sophistication. On the web, clarity beats elegance. |

## Code Style

N/A — Steve Krug's domain is usability, interaction design, and information architecture, not software development. He has never published code or coding guidelines. ## Contrarian Takes

**Three users is enough for a usability test.**
The UX research establishment historically demanded large sample sizes, controlled lab environments, and statistical validity. Krug says three users per round, tested monthly, is more valuable than one big formal study, because the binding constraint is repair capacity, not discovery: "You can find more problems in a day than you can fix in a month". This was considered borderline heretical when he first advocated it.

**Users don't read — they scan, satisfice, and muddle through.**
While designers obsess over copy, information architecture, and user journeys, Krug insists users barely engage with any of it. They don't read pages, don't make optimal choices, and don't understand how things work. They take the first reasonable option and muddle through with wrong mental models. Design for this reality rather than your fantasy of a careful, attentive user.

**Usability testing doesn't require expertise.**
The prevailing view was that usability testing required trained professionals, specialised labs, and significant budgets. Krug argues almost anyone can run a useful test in a conference room with a laptop and screen-sharing: "That's the point of Rocket Surgery Made Easy: to get people to do their own usability testing, to make it clear that this is something that's so simple you can do it yourself and it doesn't have to take a lot of time." His entire second book exists to prove this point.

**Don't fix it properly — tweak it.**
The instinct is to solve problems completely — redesign the navigation, rebuild the information architecture, rethink the user flow. Krug's answer: "I'm trying to get people to think in terms of tweaking rather than redesigning. Do as little as possible. You don't have to really fix it." This offends designers who want elegant solutions, but Krug argues that a tweak shipped today beats a redesign shipped never.

**Usability arguments can't be won with logic — only with testing.**
When teams argue about design, everyone projects their own preferences onto users. These religious debates are unresolvable through discussion. The only antidote is watching real users, which often reveals that the thing being argued about didn't matter — the real problem was something nobody was discussing.

**Accessibility is a moral issue, not a business case.**
Krug breaks with the usual pragmatic framing (legal risk, market size, SEO benefits) and argues straight from ethics: "You should do this because it's the right thing. It improves people's lives." He finds the standard business arguments unconvincing, especially to young able-bodied people, and goes for the emotional gut punch instead.

**UX didn't really exist before the iPhone.**
While many trace UX history to Don Norman's coining of the term in the 1990s, Krug has said that when he wrote the book UX didn't exist, and that it only really came into being with the iPhone — because Steve Jobs proved you could make money by doing user experience work. Before that, it was hard to convince a big company to spend on it.

## Worked Examples

### Scenario 1: The Team Can't Agree on the Navigation

**Problem**: A product team has been debating the site navigation for three weeks. The designer wants a minimalist hamburger menu. The product manager wants all eight sections visible. The CEO wants the company blog prominently featured. Nobody will budge.

**Their approach**: Stop — this is a religious debate. Everyone in the room is projecting their own preferences onto users and assuming they represent everyone. You cannot resolve it by arguing; you can only resolve it by watching real people try to use the site. So: next Tuesday morning, get three people in. Give them tasks — find the pricing page, contact support, read the latest blog post — and watch what happens. You'll almost certainly discover that the thing you're arguing about isn't the real problem, and that users can't even tell what your company does. Fix that first, then worry about the hamburger menu.

**Conclusion**: Testing replaces opinion with evidence. It almost always reveals that the real problems are different from what the team was debating.

### Scenario 2: The Landing Page Isn't Converting

**Problem**: A startup's landing page has a 15% bounce rate increase after a redesign. The team added a storytelling approach with a long narrative about the company's mission, beautiful full-bleed photography, and a CTA buried below three scrolls of content.

**Their approach**: You've designed for the brochure reader, but your users are driving past a billboard at 60 miles an hour. They scanned this page for about half a second, didn't immediately see what you do or why they should care, and hit the Back button. Apply the basics. Can a user tell what this site is and what they can do here within five seconds? If not, that's the problem. The mission statement is happy talk — cut it. Make the value proposition visible without scrolling. Make the CTA obvious. Then apply the third law: get rid of half the words, then get rid of half of what's left. The beautiful narrative is noise, and clarity trumps storytelling.

**Conclusion**: Strip away everything that doesn't help users immediately understand what they can do here. Design for scanners, not readers.

### Scenario 3: The Redesign That Never Ships

**Problem**: A company has been planning a complete website redesign for 18 months. The current site has known usability problems — confusing checkout flow, unclear error messages, broken search — but the team keeps deferring them to the redesign.

**Their approach**: You're letting known problems that are costing you money persist while you work on a redesign that may never finish. Forget the redesign for now. Take the three worst problems your users face — you probably already know what they are — and fix each with the smallest tweak possible. Confusing error message? Rewrite it in plain language; that's a one-hour fix. Checkout flow unclear? Add a progress indicator and clearer button labels, rather than redesigning the whole flow. You can ship these this week, and users benefit immediately. If you still want the redesign afterwards, fine — but stop treating it as an excuse not to fix what's broken today.

**Conclusion**: Tweak, don't redesign. Ship small fixes now. Stop using the promise of a future redesign as permission to ignore current pain.

### Scenario 4: The Client Wants to Add More Content to Every Page

**Problem**: A client keeps requesting more content on every page — legal disclaimers, promotional banners, factoids, social media feeds, newsletter signup popups. Each request seems reasonable individually, but pages are becoming cluttered and overwhelming.

**Their approach**: Every element you add competes with everything else on the page. It's a zero-sum game: that promotional banner doesn't just add itself, it makes everything else harder to find. Users don't read pages, they scan them, so each new element is noise that hides the signal. The rule: for every new element someone wants to add, ask what they're willing to remove. If nothing, the answer is no. Then apply the trunk test — grab a random internal page, pretend you've just arrived from a search engine, and try to answer the basic questions. If the noise makes that hard, there's too much on the page. Get rid of half the words, then get rid of half of what's left.

**Conclusion**: Every addition has a cost. Make teams confront the trade-off directly. Apply the trunk test to keep pages functional despite organisational pressure to add more.

### Scenario 5: Introducing Usability Testing to a Sceptical Organisation

**Problem**: A UX designer at a mid-size company wants to start usability testing but faces resistance. Management thinks it's expensive. Developers think it's a waste of time. The product owner says they already know their users.

**Their approach**: Don't ask permission, don't write a proposal, and don't try to convince anyone with arguments. Just do it. Get one person — a friend, a colleague from another department — sit them in front of your site, give them a task, and watch. Record it if you can, then show the recording to the sceptics. Nothing converts people like watching a real human struggle with something the team thought was obvious; that moment of realising what you assumed people would do isn't what they actually do beats any argument. Start with one morning, three people, and invite anyone who'll come and watch. Keep it simple so you'll keep doing it. Once people see it work, they'll want more.

**Conclusion**: Don't argue for testing — demonstrate it. One session with real users is more persuasive than any slide deck. Keep the process simple enough that it becomes a habit, not an event.

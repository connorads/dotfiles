# Feel and feedback

## Trace intention to perception

Inspect player intent, recorded input, relevant state, the simulated result and
what is rendered or heard. Small differences that the engine distinguishes may
be invisible to a player. Find which link explains the complaint before changing
damage, speed or effect intensity.

An orbiting dog's circular damage and separately looping jaw can both work as
implemented while implying different attacks. Inspect active times, facing,
reach, repeat-hit gating and the visible contact. For a presentation-only
comparison, keep damage times and hit geometry identical and display those
actual events. A directional, discrete bite is a different mechanical candidate:
compare it as such. An equal nominal period cannot preserve a continuous field's
hit opportunities, and single-target damage cannot establish crowd equivalence.
Ask players to predict contact and explain a miss. Collision tests answer a
separate question.

For tactics, make the acting unit, inspected unit, destination and target
distinguishable throughout a staged action. Let the player revise an intention
where the rules allow it. Preserve uncertainty about outcomes that belongs to
the game; clarify what the player is committing to.

## Separate response phases

Measure initial acknowledgement, build-up, active action and recovery separately.
A heavy vehicle can begin responding promptly while turning slowly. A charged
attack can communicate commitment before dealing damage. Do not equate
responsiveness with shorter completion or prescribe one latency threshold for
every device and genre.

Tune movement together with its surroundings. Speed and turning gain meaning
relative to obstacle size, spacing, visibility and traversal goals. A spatial
comparison may reveal more than another movement constant.

## Give presentation a purpose

Name the intended perception: weight, danger, material, active range, success,
character or atmosphere. Coordinate animation, sound, camera and state around
that perception. An isolated effect can look impressive while contradicting the
interaction. Inspect motion and crowded scenes, not only still images.

Match physical requirements to the platform. Competing hand actions can create
tension in embodied play; the same demands may become opaque button chores
elsewhere. Test the actual control arrangement and intended player conditions.

Swink's central game-feel model concerns direct real-time control. Its movement
toy tests are not acceptance gates for every turn-based or narrative game.
Use the parts that answer the actual experience question.

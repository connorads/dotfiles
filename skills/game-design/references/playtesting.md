# Experiments and evidence

## Diagnose without pretending to know

Separate what was reported, what was observed and the explanation being proposed.
"Boring" can mean predictable, hard to understand, aimless, repetitive, badly
paced or simply unsuitable for that player. Do not choose a cause from the word
alone. Look for the attempted action, prediction and moment that prompted it.

For a quiet observatory opening, distinguish a player studying details from one
who cannot identify anything actionable. A few minutes without a reward is not
itself a failure. Test a legible clue or altered sequence while preserving the
intended mood before concluding that an arrow, collectible or shorter duration
is needed. A concrete provisional change is useful; claiming it fixes a measured
cause when no cause was measured is not.

A useful recommendation separates these parts in its reasoning: "The report
establishes X. Y and Z could explain it. I recommend trying A because it could
produce B. Observe C to decide whether to keep it." Use ordinary prose rather
than mandatory headings. Do not open with "the actual problem is Y" and attach
uncertainty only at the end.

## Give the next test a decision

State the uncertainty, the contrasting explanations and the observation that
would change the next action. Keep unrelated tuning stable where possible. A
fixed seed does not guarantee the same encounters if changed rules consume
randomness differently; compare recorded schedules or control the relevant
streams when attribution depends on it.

Use a narrow probe for a rule, timing or balance question. Use an integrated
sequence for appeal, tone and pacing. Test the fidelity the question needs, and
report what the test omits. Do not make access to a human tester a reason to stop
preparing the requested playable work.

Give prototype numbers a purpose and label them as starting values. Timing and
success thresholds depend on the audience and experience. A few sessions can
guide a revision; they do not estimate population preference or establish a
universal release gate.

## Watch the right people

Fresh players reveal first-encounter learning. Returning players reveal mastery
and later depth. Social play requires the intended group. Observe before
explaining, then ask about specific moments without leading the answer. Constant
think-aloud can change action play; use retrospective questions when appropriate.

For puzzles, distinguish discovering the solution from executing a known one.
Repeated reasonable but rejected attempts may reveal unclear rules or a missing
affordance. A larger hint panel addresses neither automatically.

For narrative, examine what players notice, infer and feel, without requiring
everyone to produce the same interpretation. For comfort, distinguish a valued
ritual from unwanted labour. For a mastery game, examine changed tactics and
understood outcomes. Ask about replay only when replay belongs to the ambition.

## Report the evidence accurately

Bots can establish reachability, bounded behaviour or viability under their
policies. Browser checks can establish interface behaviour. Screenshots can
establish visible layout. None alone demonstrates enjoyment or comprehension.

Record enough to reproduce a finding: version, starting setup, relevant actions,
observed outcome and the uncertainty left open. If making a comparison, retain
both setups and the actual differences. Keep playable builds and their launch
instructions available for the user's later review.

Finish with a recommendation, its reason and the next useful observation. The
test serves a design decision; avoid turning every creative choice into a
statistical experiment or a mandatory permission checkpoint.

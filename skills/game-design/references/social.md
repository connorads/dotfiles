# Social play

## Design the relationship

Specify what players know about one another, what they want from one another and
which actions expose, help or frustrate them. Multiplayer connectivity is not a
social design. Choose the intended group size, screen arrangement and skill
spread before evaluating the experience.

Show how private roles, identities and goals reach their owners without leaking
on a shared screen. Use hardware actually present; a gamepad does not imply a
private display. An honour-based look-away reveal is possible, but state that
social requirement and include setup time. Account for what others can infer
from the controller, cursor or reveal procedure. A "secret card" is not
implementable until that channel exists.

One useful mechanism for deception is conflicting incentives. Advancing towards
a goal can expose a hidden identity; blending in protects it but delays success.
Specify the clues, commitment and resolution that let other people infer and
respond. Avoid making the optimal strategy perpetual inaction.

Shared construction can create temporary cooperation and rivalry. Players may
need a route everyone can use while wanting an advantage on it. Trace why each
participant would help, obstruct or retaliate under the scoring rules. Do not
rely on hoped-for banter to rescue incentives that reward doing nothing.

## Keep the group in the game

Account for skill differences through the actual rules: roles, information,
complementary abilities, alternative goals or a suitable mix of uncertainty and
execution. Explain the trade-off rather than assuming randomness makes play fair.
Avoid erasing mastery when mastery is part of the promise.

Simple controls remove some execution demands, not differences in inference,
attention or confidence. Walk a novice and expert through the same incentives.
If a catch-up rule exposes the leader or removes their agency, assess that
player's experience too; a close score is not sufficient evidence of a good rule.

Specify what happens after a mistake or elimination. Short rounds, useful
remaining roles or a quick return can preserve participation. Spectating can
also be enjoyable when players have something to anticipate or influence; test
this with the intended group.

For an expressive sport, trace a beginner action and an advanced use of the same
rules. Timing, geometry and resource costs can support mastery without an upgrade
catalogue. Specify the terminal condition: an unresolved final action can create
a dramatic finish if both players can understand and affect it.

## Observe a whole round

Write one round from setup through commitment, consequences and reset. Test with
people in the intended arrangement. Observe bluffing, advice, rivalry, laughter,
withdrawal and what players recount afterwards. These are observations to
interpret, not a requirement that every player react identically. Include quiet
or less-skilled players in the assessment.

# Sources and limits

These sources support design lenses, not universal laws or proof that a proposed
game is engaging. Chapter and section names identify the book passages; printed
pages further identify Sellers and Lovell. The guidance is an original synthesis.

## Books

| Source | Relevant passages | Use and limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Michael Sellers, *Advanced Game Design: A Systems Approach* | Ch. 5, "Analysing Games from a Systems View", pp. 182-184; ch. 6, pp. 215-217; ch. 7, "The Four Principal Loops", pp. 224-227, and pp. 258-260; ch. 10, pp. 320-327; ch. 12, pp. 387-394 | Move between experience, systems and rules; inspect learning and feedback loops; trace resources; separate prototype questions from player goals. Complexity depends on the intended experience. In the inspected PDF, viewer page = printed page +16. |
| Brenda Brathwaite and Ian Schreiber, *Challenges for Games Designers* | Ch. 3, "Puzzle Design", "All for One and One for All", "Level Design and Puzzle Design"; ch. 6, "Types of Decisions"; ch. 12, "Learning an Unfamiliar Genre"; ch. 13, "Designing a Game to Tell a Story"; ch. 14, "Core Check", "After the Tinkering" | Analyse references as player and designer; distinguish theme from genre; inspect choices and puzzle attempts; recombine or change mechanics. Frequent strategic decisions are not a requirement for every narrative or contemplative work. |
| Steve Swink, *Game Feel* | Ch. 1, "Examples"; ch. 5, "Soft Metrics vs. Hard Metrics"; ch. 7, "Attack, Decay, Sustain and Release"; ch. 8, "Medium-Level Context"; ch. 9, "Types of Polish"; ch. 17, "Predictable Results", "Instantaneous Response", "Harmony" | Inspect input, state, spatial context and perceived feedback. The central model concerns direct real-time control. Its toy tests and latency examples are not cross-genre acceptance gates. |
| Raph Koster, *A Theory of Fun for Game Design* | Ch. 3, "What Games Are"; ch. 5, "What Games Aren't"; ch. 7, "The Problem with Learning" | Distinguish reasons for boredom and examine rewarded behaviour. The book's learning/mastery definition of fun is narrower than all valuable play; ch. 5 recognises story, comfort, meditation and other purposes. |
| Nicholas Lovell, *The Pyramid of Game Design* | Ch. 4, "The Core Loop and the Gearbox"; ch. 5, pp. 77-82; ch. 6, pp. 91-93; ch. 11, pp. 177-194, especially "Using the Scientific Method" and "The Pivot Meeting" | Test the connection between immediate activity and longer-term progress where both matter; match prototypes to uncertainty. Service-game retention, monetisation and short-session assumptions remain conditional. |
| Robert Nystrom, *Game Programming Patterns* | "Architecture, Performance, and Games", especially "The Good in Bad Code", "At What Cost?", "Striking a Balance" | Recognise iteration costs and distinguish experimental work from retained implementation. Engineering patterns belong in engineering guidance, not this design skill. |

## Reconcile disagreements

- Sellers and Lovell use "core loop" differently. Specify actions, connections
  and timescales rather than assuming a shared definition.
- Prototype disposal is an engineering decision. Preserve the learning objective
  without prescribing either a universal rewrite or permanent retention of code.
- Do not convert demographic or learning-style generalisations into player models.
  Observe the intended audience.
- Balance, novelty, retention and mechanical depth are conditional means. Neither
  a book nor an acclaimed game overrides the user's chosen experience.
- The observatory, courier and companion examples in the references are worked
  hypotheses. They do not report successful player tests.
